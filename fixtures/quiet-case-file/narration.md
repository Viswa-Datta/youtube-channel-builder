# How a Cold Case Investigation Actually Works, From First Report to Final Arrest

<!-- scene 0: Cold open -->
A cold case rarely begins with anything dramatic. It begins with paperwork. A phone call is logged. An officer writes a report. Someone is assigned. A few days later there are interviews, a list of names, a box of evidence with a number written on the side. And then, for reasons that are usually ordinary rather than mysterious, the work slows down. The leads run out. The detective is pulled onto newer cases. The box goes onto a shelf.

Years pass. Sometimes decades. The people who knew the victim grow older. Some of them move away. Some of them die. And the question that started the file is still sitting there, unanswered.

This video is about what happens next. Not in a single famous case, but in the process itself. How does a case go cold? Who decides to open it again? What actually changes between the first investigation and the second one? And what has to be true, on paper and in court, before anyone can say a cold case has been solved?

To keep this concrete, we are going to follow a composite case. It is not a real case, and the people in it are not real people. Every step we describe is drawn from published guidance, from the National Institute of Justice, the Department of Justice, the FBI, and the public court process. Where something is a matter of policy, we will tell you whose policy. Where something is disputed, we will say so. And where something simply is not known, we will say that too.

Here is the composite case. A woman in her thirties is reported missing by her sister in a mid-sized American city. Two days later, her body is found. The local police department opens a homicide investigation. Detectives collect physical evidence from the scene, including biological material that might one day carry a DNA profile. They interview family, neighbors and co-workers. They build a timeline of her last known movements. They identify a handful of people worth a closer look. None of it is enough to charge anyone.

Within a year, the investigation has stalled. Within five years, the original lead detective has retired. The evidence is still in storage. The file is still open. In the language of the police department, it is unsolved. In the language most of us use, it has gone cold.

There is no single national definition of a cold case. Different agencies draw the line in different places. Some use a fixed amount of time with no active leads. Others use the moment the original investigators are reassigned. What the definitions share is a simple idea. The case is unresolved, and nobody is currently working it.

That gap, between unresolved and abandoned, is where this story lives. Because in many agencies, a cold case is not a closed case. It is a paused one. The evidence was kept. The reports were written. The names were recorded. And all of that can be picked up again, by different people, with different tools, if someone decides it is worth doing.

Over the next half hour, we will walk through that second investigation, step by step. We will look at how the original timeline is rebuilt from the record, what the first response would have captured, how a cold case team decides what to test and who to talk to, how a DNA database search actually works, where genetic genealogy fits in and where it does not, what a court needs before a case can move forward, and what remains genuinely unsettled about all of it.

We will also spend time on the people the process is supposed to serve. The families who wait. The witnesses who are asked to remember. And the person who is eventually accused, who is entitled to the same careful standard of proof as anyone else.

This is how a cold case investigation actually works.

<!-- scene 1: The timeline, sourced -->
Every serious cold case review starts in the same place. Not with a new theory, but with the old file, read slowly from the first page.

In our composite case, the file is a few thousand pages. The initial incident report. Supplementary reports from each detective. Interview notes and recorded statements. Photographs and sketches of the scene. Property and evidence logs. Laboratory requests and results. Tips that came in by phone. A few newspaper clippings that someone printed and filed. The job of the reviewing investigator is to turn that pile back into a timeline that can be trusted.

That word, trusted, matters. A timeline in a cold case is not a story. It is a list of events, each one tied to a source. The sister reported her missing at a recorded time. A neighbor said he saw her car at a certain hour. A receipt places her at a store. A phone record shows a call. When two sources disagree, the timeline does not pick a winner. It records both, and it notes the conflict.

The National Institute of Justice, the research arm of the United States Department of Justice, published a guide in July of 2019 called National Best Practices for Implementing and Sustaining a Cold Case Investigation Unit. It was written with a working group of experienced cold case investigators, forensic scientists and prosecutors. It sets out twenty-three recommendations, grouped into five broad areas, from deciding whether an agency needs a cold case unit at all to how such a unit should operate day to day. One theme runs through the whole document. Before anything new is tested, the old case has to be understood, organized and, in the guide's language, modernized.

Modernizing a case file sounds administrative, and it is. It means scanning paper, indexing names, cross-referencing every person who appears in any report, and listing every item of evidence along with where it is stored today. It means finding out whether the evidence still exists at all. Evidence can be lost, damaged, or destroyed under a retention schedule. Any honest review has to account for that before it promises anything.

While the file is being rebuilt, a second timeline comes into view. Not the timeline of the crime, but the timeline of the tools that might be used to solve it.

When our composite case first went cold, the investigators may have had access to DNA testing, but nothing like what exists now. The FBI's Combined DNA Index System, known as CODIS, began as a pilot software project in nineteen ninety, serving a small number of state and local laboratories. The DNA Identification Act of nineteen ninety-four gave the FBI formal authority to establish a national DNA index for law enforcement purposes. That national level, the National DNA Index System, became operational in nineteen ninety-eight.

Those dates matter to a cold case for a simple reason. A crime that was investigated before the database existed could never have produced a database match. A crime that was investigated soon after may have produced a profile that was never uploaded, or a profile from an older method that cannot be compared with today's records. Part of a cold case review is asking, for every piece of evidence, what could have been done with it then, and what could be done with it now.

The tools keep changing. The National Institute of Justice notes that improved DNA methods can now work with evidence that is old, degraded, or very limited, and that samples which once gave inconclusive results may now produce usable profiles. And in two thousand and nineteen, the Department of Justice issued an interim policy on a newer technique, forensic genetic genealogy, which we will come to later.

So by the end of this first stage, the reviewing investigator has two timelines side by side. One describes what happened to the victim, as far as the record can show it. The other describes what science and policy now allow. The gaps between them are where the second investigation will begin.

Before we go further, we need to talk about the first hours of an investigation, and about the recordings that may or may not exist from them.

Today, in many American police departments, officers wear body-worn cameras, and patrol cars carry dashboard cameras. When an officer responds to a call, those cameras can capture the scene as it was found, the first conversations with witnesses, and the decisions made in the first minutes. For an investigation that goes cold years later, that footage can be one of the most valuable records in the file. It shows what was there before anyone had a theory.

In our composite case, the first response happened at a time when body cameras were not widespread, so the record is the officers' written reports and the scene photographs. In a more recent case, a cold case reviewer would expect to find video, and would watch all of it, not just the parts that were flagged at the time.

There is a second question that matters for anyone telling these stories in public, including this channel. When can that footage be shown to the public at all?

The honest answer is that it depends on where the case happened. There is no single national rule for releasing body camera footage. Release policies are set by state law and by individual agencies, and they vary a great deal. Some states treat the footage as a public record with exemptions. Some restrict release while an investigation is open. Some require the consent of the people recorded, or limit what can be shown when the footage was recorded inside a private home. The Bureau of Justice Assistance maintains a body-worn camera toolkit for agencies writing these policies, and public release is one of the areas it covers.

That is why this channel follows a few firm rules. We only use footage that an agency or a court has officially released, and we go to that original release rather than to someone else's edited upload. We never use leaked, sealed or unreleased material, or footage from juvenile proceedings. And when footage is released with an agency's redactions, we keep those redactions exactly as they are.

We also add our own. Faces of bystanders and witnesses who did not consent to appear are blurred. So are victims in distress, children, license plates, house numbers, addresses, phone numbers and documents on screen. Graphic injuries are not shown at all. Where a moment matters to the story but cannot be shown with dignity, we cut away and describe it in words instead.

For this video, we did not use any real body camera footage, because our case is a composite and there is no real response to show. Where you would normally see that footage, you are seeing documents and reconstructions of the record instead, and they are labelled as such.

Back to the investigation. What does the first response contribute to a cold case, beyond video? Mostly, it contributes the boundaries of the scene and the chain of custody. Which items were collected, by whom, at what time, how they were packaged, and where they went next. A cold case can be solved decades later with a single item of evidence, but only if the record shows that the item is what it claims to be and has not been contaminated or confused with something else.

In our composite case, the reviewing investigator finds the original evidence log. It lists clothing, a swab taken at autopsy, fingernail scrapings, and several items from the place where the victim was found. Each entry has a date, a collecting officer and a storage location. Two items are marked as sent to the state laboratory in the original year, with a result recorded as no profile obtained. Three items were never sent at all. Those three lines on an old form are, at this point, the most important words in the file.

<!-- scene 3: What the investigation found -->
With the file rebuilt and the evidence accounted for, the second investigation can begin in earnest. In many departments, this work happens inside a dedicated cold case unit. The National Institute of Justice guide recommends that such units be operationally separate, staffed by experienced investigators assigned to cold cases full time, with ready access to forensic laboratory support. In smaller agencies, the same work may fall to a single detective, or to a regional team shared between departments.

The first decision is case selection. No unit can work every unsolved case at once, so most use some form of review to decide which files have the best chance of progressing. The questions are practical. Does evidence still exist that could be tested with newer methods? Are there witnesses who are still alive and could be interviewed again? Has something changed, such as a relationship, that might make a witness willing to say more than before? Our composite case scores well on the first question. There are three untested items and two that could be retested.

The second step is laboratory work. The reviewing investigator works with the laboratory to decide which items to submit, and in what order, because testing can consume the sample. The fingernail scrapings and one item of clothing are submitted. This time, using current methods, the laboratory develops a DNA profile from the scrapings that does not belong to the victim.

What happens next is a database search. A profile developed by a local laboratory is searched at the local level, and if it meets the requirements, it can be uploaded to the state level and then to the national level of CODIS. Profiles flow upward in that order, from local to state to national. If the profile matches a profile already in the system, from a convicted offender, an arrestee where state law allows, or evidence from another case, the system reports a candidate match. That match is then confirmed by the laboratories involved before it is treated as an investigative lead.

The National Institute of Justice guide makes a point that sounds obvious but has real consequences. Database hits often arrive long after an investigation began, and a unit needs to be ready to move quickly when one does. A match that sits unread in an inbox is not progress.

In our composite case, the national search returns nothing. The profile is not in the database. This is where many cold cases stopped, until recently.

Forensic genetic genealogy is a different approach. Instead of looking for the person who left the DNA, it looks for their relatives. A laboratory produces a much more detailed genetic profile from the evidence, and that profile is compared against genealogy databases whose users have chosen to allow law enforcement matching. If distant relatives are found, genealogists build family trees from public records to narrow the possibilities down to a small number of people.

In two thousand and nineteen, the Department of Justice issued an interim policy on this technique for its own agencies and for work funded by the Department. It was approved in September and took effect on the first of November. Under that policy, the technique is reserved for unsolved violent crimes and for identifying the remains of suspected homicide victims. Other available methods, including a CODIS search, are expected to be tried first. The policy also includes a section that limits how a genetic association can be used as the basis for an arrest.

That last point is important. A genealogy result is a lead, not an identification. In our composite case, the family tree points to one man who lived in the city at the time. Investigators now have a name. They do not yet have proof.

So they do what investigators have always done. They check where he lived and worked. They re-interview the original witnesses, carefully, without suggesting the answer. And they seek a direct DNA comparison, either from a sample he provides voluntarily or from one obtained lawfully, so that the laboratory can compare his profile directly with the evidence. In our composite case, that direct comparison is a match.

<!-- scene 4: Court and official record -->
A laboratory match feels like an ending. In the legal system, it is closer to a beginning.

The first question is whether the case can be charged. That decision belongs to a prosecutor, not to the police. The prosecutor will look at the whole case, not just the DNA. What does the match actually prove? A profile under a victim's fingernails can be powerful evidence, but the defense will ask how it got there, when, and whether there is an innocent explanation. The prosecutor will want to know what else supports the case. The timeline. The witnesses. Anything the suspect has said. Whether the evidence was handled properly from the day it was collected.

If the prosecutor decides to proceed, the public record begins to fill in. An arrest warrant is usually supported by a sworn document, often called a probable cause affidavit, which sets out the basis for the arrest. A charging document is filed. The case receives a docket number, and from that point, filings, hearings and rulings are recorded by the court.

For anyone researching a case, including this channel, the court record is the strongest source available, and it comes first. It is not perfect. An affidavit is one side's account, written before the defense has responded. But it is sworn, it is dated, and it can be checked against everything that follows. A news report that summarizes an affidavit is useful. The affidavit itself is better. A video about a case, including this one, should be the last place you take a fact from, not the first.

There is also a statistical side to the word solved, and it is narrower than many people realise. In the FBI's Uniform Crime Reporting program, an offence is cleared by arrest when at least one person has been arrested, charged with the offence, and turned over to the court for prosecution. That is a reporting definition. It does not mean the person has been convicted. A case counts as cleared on the day of the charge, whatever happens at trial.

The program also recognises a second category, cleared by exceptional means. This applies when elements beyond law enforcement's control prevent an arrest and charge, even though the agency has identified the offender and has enough evidence to support a charge. The most common example is that the offender has died. In cold cases, where decades may have passed, this is not unusual. A family may be told that a case has been cleared, and still never see the inside of a courtroom.

In our composite case, the suspect is arrested and charged. The case is now, in reporting terms, cleared by arrest. It is not over. He is presumed innocent. His lawyers will receive the evidence and challenge it. They may question the original collection, the storage of the evidence over many years, the laboratory methods, and the use of genetic genealogy to identify him in the first place. Those challenges are part of how the system is supposed to work, and they are one reason why careful documentation in the original investigation matters so much.

Whatever the outcome, the court record will hold the answer. A plea, a verdict, a dismissal. If this were a real case, that record is what we would show you, with personal details redacted, rather than anyone's opinion about it.

<!-- scene 5: What remains disputed / unknown -->
Every investigation leaves questions behind. Some are about a particular case. Others are about the process itself. In this chapter, we want to separate what is settled from what is still being argued about, and from what nobody actually knows.

Start with genetic genealogy. Its use by law enforcement is established, but it is not settled. Critics raise privacy concerns that go beyond any single case. A person who has never taken a DNA test can still be identified through relatives who have. People who uploaded their DNA to learn about their ancestry may not have expected it to be used in criminal investigations, and the terms of those services have changed over time. Supporters point to violent crimes that would otherwise remain unsolved, and to families who finally received answers.

The rules vary depending on where a case happens. The Department of Justice policy governs its own work and work it funds. Some states have passed their own laws. Maryland and Montana were among the first to regulate law enforcement use of genetic genealogy. Separately, some jurisdictions restrict familial searching of their own DNA databases, which is a related but different technique. Maryland and the District of Columbia prohibit it, partly because of concerns about who is over-represented in those databases. If you hear that a case was solved with genetic genealogy, the right follow-up question is under which rules.

Next, numbers. How many cold cases are there in the United States? We looked for a single authoritative national count and did not find one. Agencies define cold cases differently, record them differently, and are not required to report them in a single national format. Estimates exist, but they are built on different assumptions. So we are marking this one, plainly, as unknown.

Then there is the gap between being cleared and being convicted. As we saw in the last chapter, a case can be cleared when someone is charged, or cleared by exceptional means when no one is charged at all. Clearance figures are useful, but they are not the same thing as courtroom outcomes, and they should not be presented as if they were.

There is also a risk that runs the other way. A cold case carries pressure. Families have waited. Investigators have invested years. A new lead can feel like the answer before it has been tested. History includes cases where certainty arrived too early and later proved wrong. Good cold case practice builds in checks against this. The evidence is re-examined rather than assumed. Witness interviews are conducted without suggesting answers. The laboratory confirms rather than assumes. And the defense is given the full file, including the parts that do not fit.

Finally, there is what remains unknown inside any single case, even a solved one. In our composite case, a match tells us whose DNA was found. It does not, by itself, tell us everything about what happened that night, or why. Some of those questions may be answered at trial. Some may never be answered at all. We think it is more respectful, to the victim and to the audience, to say that clearly than to fill the silence with guesses.

So when we tell a story on this channel, you will hear three kinds of statements. Things that are confirmed by the record. Things that are disputed, and by whom. And things that are unknown. We mark them that way in our research notes, and we try to make the difference audible in the narration as well.

<!-- scene 6: Aftermath and closing -->
For the people closest to a case, the end of an investigation does not feel like a line in a report. It feels like a phone call, or a knock on the door, or a meeting in an office with someone they may not have spoken to in years.

The National Institute of Justice guide gives real attention to this part of the work. It discusses victim-centered and trauma-informed approaches, which in practice mean keeping families informed without making promises, explaining what can and cannot be shared while a case is active, and recognizing that reopening a case reopens a loss. For a family, a call from a cold case unit can bring hope and fear in the same sentence. Good units plan for that.

The guide also discusses the media. Public attention can produce new tips. It can also produce rumours, pressure, and misinformation that makes a case harder to prosecute. For that reason, many agencies limit what they say while a case is open, even when families and the public want more. Channels like this one have a responsibility here too. A story told carelessly can harm a family, mislead a jury pool, or point suspicion at the wrong person.

Then there is the question of whether cold case work lasts. The guide's own title includes the word sustaining, and for good reason. Cold case units are often created with short-term grants, and some are wound down when the money ends. A case that is reviewed once and then put back on the shelf is not much better off than one that was never reviewed. The guide's recommendations are aimed at making this work a permanent part of how an agency operates, rather than a project that comes and goes.

Let us go back, one last time, to the composite case. A woman was reported missing by her sister. The original investigators collected evidence and wrote careful reports. They did not solve the case. Years later, a different team read every page, found three items that had never been tested, and used methods that did not exist the first time. A database search came up empty. A newer technique produced a name. Traditional investigation and a direct DNA comparison turned that name into evidence. A prosecutor brought a charge, and a court took it from there.

None of the people in that story are real. But every step is. And the step that mattered most was not the newest technology. It was the first one. Someone, at the very beginning, packaged the evidence properly, wrote down where it went, and made sure it would still be there when better tools arrived.

That is the quiet truth at the centre of most cold cases. They are not usually solved by a single brilliant insight. They are solved by records that were kept, by evidence that was preserved, and by people who were willing to read an old file slowly, from the first page to the last.
